## Signs
- A cut is a break or separation in the skin, often caused by a sharp object
- Minor cuts may bleed lightly and stop on their own
- Deeper cuts can cause significant bleeding and may damage deeper structures such as nerves, tendons, or blood vessels

## Do
- Check that the scene is safe and use gloves if available
- Apply firm, direct pressure with clean cloth, gauze, or a dressing to control bleeding
- Maintain pressure until bleeding is controlled
- Once bleeding is controlled, clean a minor wound with clean running water or sterile saline
- Remove visible, easily accessible dirt or debris if possible
- Cover the wound with a clean dressing
- Change the dressing when it becomes wet or dirty
- Monitor for signs of infection

## Don't
- Don't repeatedly remove a dressing to check whether bleeding has stopped
- Don't remove an embedded object; apply pressure around it instead
- Don't use hydrogen peroxide or iodine routinely inside a wound
- Don't delay emergency care when bleeding is severe or cannot be controlled

## Seek emergency care when
- Blood is rapidly flowing or spurting
- Bleeding continues despite firm direct pressure
- The wound is deep, large, or gaping
- There is an embedded object that cannot safely be removed
- There may be damage to a tendon, nerve, blood vessel, or other deep structure
- The person develops weakness, dizziness, fainting, pale or clammy skin, or other signs of significant blood loss or shock
- The wound is caused by a human or animal bite
- Signs of infection develop
- Tetanus protection may not be up to date, particularly for a deep or dirty wound

Sources: 2024 American Heart Association / American Red Cross Guidelines for First Aid (External Bleeding Control; Superficial Wounds and Abrasions), IFRC International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2025 (Severe Bleeding), MSD Manual (First Aid for Cuts, Scrapes, and Wounds), Mayo Clinic (Cuts and scrapes: First aid)
