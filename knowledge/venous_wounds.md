# Venous leg ulcers

## What it is
- A venous leg ulcer is an open sore on the lower leg caused by problems with venous circulation
- It commonly develops between the knee and ankle, often around the inner lower leg or ankle
- Venous ulcers are usually chronic and require professional assessment and treatment

## Signs
- An open sore that takes more than 2 weeks to heal
- Commonly located between the knee and ankle
- Swelling of the ankle or lower leg
- Darkening or discoloration of the skin around the ulcer
- Hardened or thickened skin around the wound
- A heavy, aching, or swollen feeling in the legs
- Varicose veins may be present
- Itchy, flaky, or inflamed skin associated with venous eczema
- Drainage or exudate from the ulcer

## Do
- Seek assessment by a healthcare professional if a venous leg ulcer is suspected
- Keep the ulcer clean and covered with the dressing recommended by the healthcare professional
- Follow the prescribed compression treatment exactly as instructed
- Elevate the affected leg when appropriate to help reduce swelling
- Remain physically active as tolerated and avoid prolonged periods of sitting or standing still
- Protect the affected leg from further injury
- Attend scheduled wound-care and follow-up appointments

## Don't
- Don't apply compression without appropriate assessment of arterial circulation
- Don't attempt to apply compression bandages without appropriate training and instructions
- Don't remove or change a prescribed compression system unless instructed or if urgent symptoms require removal
- Don't ignore increasing pain, swelling, colour change, or numbness while wearing compression
- Don't use antibiotics solely to make an ulcer heal; antibiotics are used when there is clinical evidence of infection
- Don't attempt aggressive debridement yourself

## Signs of infection
- Increasing pain
- Increasing redness or swelling around the ulcer
- Green, foul-smelling, or increased discharge
- Fever or feeling generally unwell
- Unpleasant smell from the wound

## Seek urgent medical advice when
- Infection is suspected
- The leg or foot becomes unusually painful, pale, blue, cold, numb, or swollen
- Compression causes numbness, tingling, unusual pain, or toes that become pale or blue
- The wound is rapidly worsening
- There is significant bleeding or other acute deterioration

## Professional management
- Assessment should include evaluation of arterial circulation before compression is started
- Treatment commonly includes appropriate wound cleaning and dressings plus compression when clinically appropriate
- Compression should be selected and applied by appropriately trained healthcare professionals
- The underlying venous disease and associated swelling should also be managed
- Follow-up is required to monitor healing and adjust treatment
