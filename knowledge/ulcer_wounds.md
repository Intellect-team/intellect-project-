# Ulcer wounds

## What it is
- An ulcer is an open area of skin or tissue loss that is slow to heal or fails to heal normally
- Ulcers can have different causes, including pressure, diabetes, venous disease, arterial disease, neuropathy, or other medical conditions
- Treatment depends on the underlying cause

## Signs
- An open sore or area of tissue loss
- A wound that does not heal normally
- Drainage or exudate from the wound
- Redness, swelling, or warmth around the wound
- Pain or tenderness, although some ulcers may cause little or no pain because of reduced sensation
- Changes in skin colour or texture around the wound
- Swelling of the surrounding limb may occur with some types of ulcers

## Do
- Seek medical assessment for a persistent or unexplained ulcer
- Keep the wound clean and covered according to professional wound-care advice
- Protect the area from further pressure, friction, or trauma
- Follow the treatment plan for the underlying cause
- Monitor the wound for changes in size, depth, drainage, colour, pain, or surrounding skin
- Seek prompt medical attention if infection or worsening tissue damage is suspected

## Don't
- Don't assume all ulcers should be treated in the same way
- Don't apply compression to a leg ulcer without appropriate assessment of arterial circulation
- Don't remove dead tissue or perform debridement yourself
- Don't apply antibiotics simply because an ulcer is present
- Don't ignore an ulcer that is worsening or failing to heal
- Don't rely only on pain to determine severity

## Possible causes
- Pressure ulcer: caused by prolonged pressure and/or shear
- Diabetic foot ulcer: associated with diabetes, often involving neuropathy, pressure, deformity, or vascular disease
- Venous leg ulcer: associated with chronic venous disease and commonly occurs around the lower leg and ankle
- Arterial ulcer: associated with inadequate arterial blood flow
- Other ulcers may result from inflammatory, traumatic, infectious, or other medical conditions

## Seek urgent medical care when
- There is rapidly spreading redness or swelling
- There is fever, systemic illness, or suspected sepsis
- The wound has rapidly worsening pain or tissue damage
- There is black or necrotic tissue
- There are signs of severe circulation problems, such as a cold, pale, blue, or severely painful limb
- There is suspected deep infection or bone involvement
- The ulcer is associated with diabetes and shows signs of infection, ischaemia, or gangrene

## Professional assessment
- Assessment should identify the likely cause of the ulcer
- Circulation, sensation, infection, wound size, depth, and surrounding skin may need to be assessed
- Treatment should target the underlying cause rather than treating all ulcers identically

Sources: NICE NG19 (Diabetic foot problems: prevention and management), NICE CG179 (Pressure ulcers: prevention and management), NHS (Venous leg ulcer: symptoms, diagnosis and treatment), IWGDF 2023 Guidelines (Diabetes-related foot disease)
