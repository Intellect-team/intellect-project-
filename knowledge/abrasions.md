## Signs
- The top layer of skin (epidermis) is scraped or damaged, usually by friction against a rough surface
- Common locations include the knees, elbows, ankles, and other areas exposed to friction
- Dirt, gravel, or other foreign material may be embedded in the wound
- Minor bleeding, pain, redness, or tenderness may occur

## Do
- Check that the scene is safe and wash your hands if possible
- Thoroughly irrigate the wound with clean running water or sterile saline
- Continue rinsing until there is no obvious dirt or foreign material in the wound
- Remove easily accessible debris with clean tweezers if necessary
- Cover a clean abrasion with a clean, non-stick or occlusive dressing to protect it and support healing
- Change the dressing when it becomes wet or dirty
- Monitor the wound for signs of infection

## Don't
- Don't aggressively scrub the wound
- Don't routinely use antiseptic agents such as povidone-iodine instead of proper irrigation
- Don't blow on the wound
- Don't leave obvious dirt or foreign material embedded in the wound

## Seek medical care when
- Dirt or foreign material cannot be completely removed
- The wound was caused by a human or animal bite or contaminated with human or animal saliva
- Redness, swelling, increasing pain, foul-smelling drainage, or fever develops
- The wound is deep, extensive, or associated with significant bleeding
- Tetanus vaccination may not be up to date, particularly for a deep or dirty wound

Sources: 2024 American Heart Association / American Red Cross Guidelines for First Aid (Superficial Wounds and Abrasions), IFRC International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2025 (Wounds and bleeding), Mayo Clinic (Cuts and scrapes: First aid)
