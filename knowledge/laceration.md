# Lacerations

## Signs
- A laceration is a cut or tear in the skin caused by an injury
- It may have smooth or jagged edges
- Bleeding can range from minor to life-threatening
- Deeper lacerations may damage nerves, tendons, muscles, or blood vessels

## Do (minor laceration)
- Check that the scene is safe and use gloves if available
- Apply direct pressure with clean gauze, cloth, or a dressing if bleeding
- Once bleeding is controlled, clean the wound with clean running water or sterile saline
- Cover the wound with a clean dressing
- Monitor for infection and other complications

## Do (severe or life-threatening bleeding)
- Activate emergency medical services
- Apply firm, continuous direct pressure to the wound
- If blood soaks through the dressing, place another dressing on top and continue applying pressure; do not remove the original dressing
- If an object is embedded in the wound, do not remove it — apply pressure around the object to control bleeding and stabilize it
- For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg that is not controlled by direct pressure, a tourniquet may be applied if available and appropriate
- A hemostatic dressing may be used for life-threatening bleeding when available, together with direct pressure
- Once a tourniquet has been applied for life-threatening extremity bleeding, do not remove it; leave it in place until emergency medical professionals take over
- Keep the person warm and monitor for signs of shock while waiting for emergency help

## Don't
- Don't remove an embedded object
- Don't remove the original blood-soaked dressing; add another dressing on top
- Don't use a tourniquet for minor bleeding
- Don't delay emergency care for severe bleeding

## Seek emergency care when
- Bleeding is severe, rapidly flowing, or spurting
- Bleeding cannot be controlled with direct pressure
- A tourniquet is required or has been applied
- The wound is deep or gaping
- An object is embedded in the wound
- There is possible injury to a nerve, tendon, muscle, or blood vessel
- The person develops dizziness, weakness, pale or clammy skin, fainting, or other signs of shock

Sources: 2024 American Heart Association / American Red Cross Guidelines for First Aid (External Bleeding Control), IFRC International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2025 (Severe Bleeding), MSD Manual (First Aid for Cuts, Scrapes, and Wounds)
